## Purpose

This skill guides an agent through designing a research program that identifies an observable or simulated discrepancy, generates competing mechanistic explanations, and validates them via minimal controlled tests, strong controls, and multi-diagnostic discrimination. It is intended for astrophysics and cosmology contexts where selection effects, model degeneracy, and numerical approximations complicate causal inference.

## When to use

- When a user presents an unexplained trend, tension between observations and models, or an anomalous structural, thermodynamic, or dynamical signature in an astrophysical system.
- When designing a new study that must distinguish multiple physical mechanisms—such as feedback modes, transport processes, or assembly channels—using observations or simulations.
- When planning a simulation campaign to test whether a proposed process resolves a multi-constraint tension without introducing new discrepancies.
- When reviewing a draft study for whether its claims follow from its evidence and controls.
- Do not use as a general-purpose astrophysics tutor, literature-review generator, or operational telescope or simulation deployment guide.

## Inputs required

- **Target phenomenon or discrepancy:** the system-level behavior, scaling-relation deviation, or model failure to explain.
- **Available evidence:** observational catalogs, survey products, simulation outputs, archival datasets, or published benchmarks.
- **Candidate mechanisms:** physical processes hypothesized to drive the discrepancy (may be supplied by the user or generated by the agent).
- **Data and simulation constraints:** resolution, sample size, redshift or mass range, selection function, computational budget, and known systematic limitations.
- **User objective:** hypothesis generation, experimental design, analysis plan, validation review, or iteration strategy.

## Core Workflow

Follow this ordered end-to-end route, applying decision gates and stop conditions at each stage.

1. **Frame the discrepancy and gap**
   - Define the observable tension or model failure. State what is expected versus seen, where it appears, and which diagnostics expose it.
   - Separate observational limitations, theoretical ambiguity, and methodological weakness.
   - **Decision gate:** If the discrepancy is not measurable with available data or simulations, stop and redefine the question or request additional inputs.

2. **Generate competing mechanistic hypotheses**
   - Produce at least three explanations: one conventional, one alternative physical mechanism, and one null, artifact, or selection-driven explanation.
   - For each, specify the causal chain (mechanism → local process → global signature → observable diagnostic), key assumptions, and evidence strength.
   - **Decision gate:** If hypotheses are not falsifiable or do not predict distinct signatures across independent diagnostics, refine them before proceeding.

3. **Design the minimal controlled test**
   - Select the smallest validation that can reject the weakest alternative: a minimal simulation, a stratified observational comparison, or a stacked time-series analysis.
   - Define study units, primary and response variables, covariates, and at least one negative control (signal absent), one positive control (signal known), and one robustness control (varying a key assumption).
   - **Decision gate:** If no decisive test can be constructed given constraints, stop and scope down the question or expand the dataset.

4. **Execute analysis with pre-registered comparisons**
   - State the analysis contract before interpreting results: primary comparison, secondary checks, and success or failure criteria.
   - Check data lineage, sample coverage, and baseline comparability.
   - Compute the primary response; decompose it into mechanistic components if applicable (for example, fuel supply versus efficiency, or thermal versus non-thermal contributions).
   - Propagate measurement, sampling, model, and systematic uncertainties.
   - **Decision gate:** If data quality is insufficient, controls fail, or the signal appears before the hypothesized trigger, halt and diagnose confounders.

5. **Discriminate mechanisms via multi-diagnostic checks**
   - Compare predictions across independent observables or simulation diagnostics (for example, thermal, dynamical, and non-thermal tracers).
   - Use paired ablations in simulations or matched controls in observations to isolate causal contributions.
   - **Decision gate:** If multiple mechanisms remain compatible after the minimal test, proceed to strong validation. If the leading hypothesis is clearly rejected, pivot to alternatives.

6. **Run strong validation and robustness checks**
   - Increase complexity only after minimal tests pass: add resolution or convergence checks, alternate definitions, broader samples, or integrated system tests.
   - Test sensitivity to selection cuts, background models, projection effects, subgrid prescriptions, and numerical approximations.
   - **Decision gate:** If the effect disappears under reasonable variations, label it as fragile or artifact-limited and stop promotion.

7. **Synthesize evidence and bound claims**
   - Assign evidence strength—verified, inferred, or speculative—to each surviving hypothesis.
   - State boundary conditions: mass range, redshift, environment, resolution, or data product where the conclusion should not be extrapolated.
   - **Decision gate:** If the mechanism improves one constraint but worsens another, treat it as incomplete and require a hybrid or revised model.

8. **Iterate or redesign**
   - If supported: broaden to adjacent populations, regimes, or model extensions.
   - If weak or null: evaluate whether sensitivity was adequate before discarding; if not, redesign for a more discriminating observable.
   - If controls fail: treat results as invalid until the workflow, data selection, or model assumptions are repaired.
   - **Stop condition:** Stop when the hypothesis is either promoted with bounded confidence, rejected by decisive evidence, or deferred due to unresolved degeneracy.

## Output Format

When using this skill, return the result in this structure:

1. **Discrepancy framing** — phenomenon, expected versus observed behavior, and key diagnostic.
2. **Evidence map** — directly measured, inferred, assumed, and unknown elements.
3. **Candidate hypotheses** — hypothesis, mechanism, predicted signatures across independent diagnostics, evidence strength, key assumptions, and likely failure modes.
4. **Experimental design** — minimal viable test, study units, variables, controls (positive, negative, robustness), and stopping conditions.
5. **Analysis plan** — primary comparison, component decomposition, uncertainty treatment, and robustness checks.
6. **Validation report** — control outcomes, convergence or resolution checks, sensitivity results, and mechanism discrimination status.
7. **Iteration plan** — promote, revise, reject, or defer decisions with the next most informative experiment.
8. **Claim boundaries and validity notes** — extrapolation limits, confounders, and reproducibility requirements.

## Validation Gates

- **Measurable discrepancy:** the tension must be expressible as a quantifiable deviation or model mismatch.
- **Falsifiable hypotheses:** each mechanism must predict a distinct observable or simulated signature that could be proven wrong.
- **Control adequacy:** every claim must be supported by at least one negative control and one robustness check.
- **Baseline comparability:** pre-event or pre-intervention systems must match on key covariates before the test.
- **Uncertainty propagation:** measurement, sampling, model, and systematic uncertainties must be reported and propagated.
- **Convergence and stability:** simulation-based claims require checks of numerical invariants and resolution sensitivity before physical interpretation.

## Claim Boundaries

- Distinguish “the mechanism changes the system state” from “the mechanism solves the original discrepancy.”
- Do not extrapolate beyond the tested mass range, redshift, environment, dynamical state, or data product.
- Mark conclusions as verified (multiple independent lines), inferred (model-dependent or single diagnostic), or speculative (analogy-driven).
- Never present a single study or simulation as field-wide consensus.

## Failure Modes

- **Selection or artifact hypothesis:** the trend arises from catalog cuts, projection effects, calibration bias, or survey incompleteness.
- **Timing confusion:** the signal precedes the proposed trigger, indicating reverse causation or pre-existing differences.
- **Numerical artifact:** the result depends on resolution, timestep, force approximation, smoothing, or subgrid prescription.
- **Model degeneracy:** multiple mechanisms produce identical thermal or structural signatures under current diagnostics.
- **Over-correction:** the proposed mechanism fixes one tension but creates a new discrepancy in an independent observable.
- **Proxy mismatch:** the diagnostic does not trace the intended physical quantity.
- **Insufficient sensitivity:** the test cannot distinguish the predicted effect from noise or systematic variation.

## Validity Checks

- **Confounding:** assess selection effects, environmental covariates, repeated events, and population mixing.
- **Reproducibility:** document data versions, selection rules, random seeds, software versions, analysis assumptions, and diagnostic definitions.
- **Statistical validity:** handle sparse groups, multiple comparisons, and uncertainty budgets conservatively.
- **Numerical validity:** verify conservation, convergence, and stability before physical interpretation in simulation studies.
- **Boundary conditions:** state regimes where the mechanism should weaken, disappear, or reverse.
- **Narrative consistency:** ensure the research question, hypothesis, design, analysis, and conclusion tell the same causal story without overstatement.
